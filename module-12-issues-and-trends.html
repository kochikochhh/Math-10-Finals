The big questions

Math is a dynamic, useful, creative human endeavor, so why do so many students fail it? Gender, socioeconomic background, race and culture all affect math learning. The module adds two more issues:

  • How can mathematics change relations in society and promote equity and social justice?
  • What is the nature of mathematical knowledge, and what have non-Western societies contributed?

1.1 Ethnomathematics: Mathematics and Culture

Ethnomathematics studies the interplay of mathematics and culture. It critiques the dominant view of Western mathematics as the mathematics, singular, universal and value-free (Hottinger, 2016). One view is that it challenges Eurocentrism in mathematics (Powell & Frankenstein, 1997).

  • Eurocentric trajectory: the usual history of math treats it as an exclusive product of European civilization (Joseph, 2009). That's where the phrase “Western mathematics” comes from.
  • Socio-cultural mathematics: the math that develops within a particular culture. Some scholars use it as another name for ethnomathematics, but ethnomathematics has a wider agenda: history, politics, philosophy, culture and pedagogy.
  • A key task: uncovering mathematical ideas embedded in cultural practices and artifacts, ideas that were hidden, frozen, subjugated or marginalized during colonization.
  • Paulus Gerdes (Mozambique) pioneered this. He and his students studied African geometry and fractals. Uncovering math in culture supports cultural affirmation and conscientization (critical awareness).
  • Alan Bishop, Western mathematics: the secret weapon of cultural imperialism (1990), challenges the myth that math is culture-free, and sees math as a pan-cultural phenomenon, found in every culture.
Link to earlier modules: the Babylonian, Egyptian, Chinese and Hindu-Arabic numeration systems (Module 5) and the symmetries in Philippine textiles like binakol (Module 8) are examples of math developed outside Europe. That's ethnomathematics in action.

1.2 Mathematics and Technology

  • Uses: computer simulations help visualize mathematical models and give new insights. Computers, graphics calculators, interactive smart boards and apps can deepen conceptual understanding when used well.
  • Issue 1, access: not every school, teacher or student can afford these tools.
  • Issue 2, teacher readiness: not every teacher is prepared to teach with them.

Borba et al. (2017), reported at the 13th International Congress on Mathematical Education (ICME-13, 2016), named five trends in math education and digital technology:

  1. Mobile technologies
  2. Massive open online courses (MOOCs)
  3. Digital libraries and designing learning objects
  4. Collaborative learning using digital technology
  5. Teacher training using blended learning

For Activity 1.2's debates, think about calculators in class (understanding vs. computation), AI tools, online learning during the pandemic, and the digital divide between city and rural schools.

2.1 Socioeconomic Status (SES) and Success in Math

According to the American Psychological Association, students from low-SES households and communities develop academic skills more slowly than those from higher-SES groups. This holds in both developing and developed countries.

  • TIMSS (Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study) measures math and science achievement at grades 4 and 8.
  • Article 5 (Caygill & Kirkham, 2008) covers New Zealand's Year 5 (grade 4) math results from 1994 to 2006, broken down by SES and home educational resources.
  • A proxy measure is something measurable that stands in for SES, which is hard to measure directly.
Common TIMSS-style proxiesAt homeNumber of books at home; a computer or internet; a study desk or own room; parents' education

2.2 Indigenous Peoples and Mathematics

Indigenous peoples have a long history of discrimination in schools. Culturally relevant education has grown worldwide; Canada, the United States, Australia and New Zealand have long histories of it. Part of the task is culturally relevant math education.

Activity 2.2: Stavros Stavrou, TEDxSaskatoon

Aboriginal math education: Collaborative learning (9:44). Discussion questions: what's different about his approach, its strengths and weaknesses, and what he means by anti-oppressive mathematics education.

In the Philippines

  • DepEd Order No. 62, s. 2011: the National Indigenous Peoples Education Policy Framework, which institutionalized IP education.
  • One policy: integrate Indigenous Knowledge Systems and Practices (IKSPs) in all learning areas, including pedagogy, content and assessment.
  • Under K-12, DepEd pursues localization and contextualization: math teachers build lessons around the local Indigenous culture.
  • PAMANA KA: a school for Indigenous Mangyan students in San Jose, Mindoro, set up before the government policy. Article 6 (Alangui, 2017) presents it as a successful model of culturally relevant math education.

3. Mathematics Education

  • Mathematics education studies how math is taught and learned, and how philosophical, psychological, historical, political, socioeconomic and cultural factors affect it.
  • Mathematical literacy (Ojose, 2011): knowing and being able to apply basic math in day-to-day life. Building a mathematically literate society is a widely shared goal.
  • The curriculum is political: what math gets taught and how is shaped by the political climate and the dominant worldview.

Critical Mathematics Education (Greer & Skovsmose, 2012)

Internal critiqueHow math is learned and taughtLooks inside the classroom: teaching methods and learning.
External critiqueMath in its contextLooks at how math and math education sit within historical, cultural, social and political contexts, and what follows from that.
  • David Stinson (2014): teaching math for social justice is an ethical and moral imperative. The math classroom is a natural place, and one of the first, to tackle injustice.
  • Eric (Rico) Gutstein (2012): Mathematics as a Weapon in the Struggle (Article 7, for the Activity 3.1 critical review).
  • The module connects this to the Philippines: strengthening democracy and social justice three decades after Martial Law.
