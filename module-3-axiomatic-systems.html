Math is like a game

A game has special terms (some defined, some not) and rules you accept without question. A baseball runner goes counterclockwise, and a queen beats a jack, because the rules say so. In mathematics, the rules are axioms, and the results built from them are theorems.

The Greeks began studying logical connections among facts around 600 B.C. Around 300 B.C., Euclid organized the known mathematics so that almost every theorem was proved from a few definitions and axioms, and the axiomatic method was born. Today no mathematical claim is accepted unless it can be proved from axioms.

4.1 The Four Components

1Undefined termsThe basic vocabulary, understood from context. Point, line, plane, set. Two kinds: elements (objects) and relations (connections between objects).
2Defined termsShortcuts for phrases used often, such as “parallel lines.” Not strictly needed, but efficient.
3AxiomsStatements accepted as true without proof. They implicitly define the undefined terms and are the starting point for proofs.
4TheoremsStatements derived from the axioms by strict logical proof.

Example: in “a point is on a line,” point and line are elements and on is a relation.

What makes a good definition

  • It states a single, unambiguous idea, its characteristic property. Given any object, you can tell whether it fits, and the term labels exactly the things that fit.
  • ✓ “An isosceles triangle is a triangle in which two sides are equal.”
  • ✗ “A square is a four-sided figure.” This is not characteristic: a rectangle or a kite also fits.
  • It must not be circular. ✗ “An obtuse triangle is a triangle that is obtuse.” ✗ number → quantity → amount → number. ✗ “Odd = not even” and “even = not odd” used together.
  • Because definitions can't be circular, some terms must stay undefined.

People need definitions even for simple shapes. Ask what a triangle is, and some will draw a shaded triangular region, while a geometer means just the three sides.

Interpretations and Models

  • Interpretation: any assignment of specific meanings to the undefined terms.
  • An interpretation satisfies an axiom if the axiom becomes true under it.
  • Model: an interpretation that satisfies all the axioms.
  • Concrete model: meanings come from the real world (dots and segments, people, objects).
  • Abstract model: meanings come from another axiomatic system (numbers, sets).

Properties of an Axiomatic System

PropertyMeaningHow to show it
Consistent
(required)		No statement and its negation are both axioms or theorems. The axioms don't contradict.Find one model where all the axioms are true.
Independent axiom
(optional)		It can't be proved from the other axioms. Replacing it with its negation still gives a consistent system.Find a model where that axiom is false and all the others are true.
Independent systemEvery axiom is independent (no redundant axioms).One such model per axiom.
CompleteAny statement about the system can be proved or disproved from the axioms.Show there is essentially one model.
CategoricalEssentially only one model (all models are isomorphic).—
Consistency is necessary; independence is not. High school geometry often takes hard theorems as postulates, so its axiom sets usually aren't independent.

Inconsistent systems

  • “A matches B” and “A does not match B”: no interpretation can make both true, so there are no models.
  • Point–line: any two points lie on exactly one line, there are exactly 4 points, each line has exactly 2 points, and there are exactly 5 lines. Four points give C(4,2) = 6 pairs, so 6 lines are needed, which contradicts “exactly 5.”

Monkey–tree system (consistent)

Exactly 3 monkeys. Every monkey climbs ≥ 2 trees. No tree is climbed by more than 2 monkeys.

Module check: the module negates Axiom 3 as “a tree climbed by at most two monkeys.” The correct negation is “there is a tree climbed by more than two monkeys,” since “no tree has more than two” is false only if some tree has three or more.

Hilbert and Gödel

  • David Hilbert (1900) posed 23 unsolved problems. His Second Problem asked for a consistent and complete set of axioms for all of mathematics.
  • Kurt Gödel (1930) proved the Incompleteness Theorem: any consistent system strong enough to describe arithmetic contains true statements that cannot be proven.
  • Example of an unproven conjecture: the Twin Primes Conjecture says there are infinitely many prime pairs that differ by 2 (3 & 5, 11 & 13, 101 & 103). A 2004 claimed proof had a flaw.

4.1.4 Theorems and Proofs

A proof is a logically sound argument from accepted ideas to the statement. Checking cases or running experiments can suggest a conjecture, but it never replaces a proof.

Ant–path, Theorem 1: There is at least one path. By Axiom 3, an ant exists. By Axiom 1, that ant has at least two paths. So at least one path exists. ■

Three-point geometry (Example 12): exactly 3 points; each two points on exactly one line; not all points on one line; each two lines share at least one point. Theorems: two lines meet in exactly one point, and there are exactly three lines.

End-of-proof marks: Q.E.D. (quod erat demonstrandum, “which was to be demonstrated”), the open “tombstone” rectangle, and today's filled square ■, called the halmos after Paul Halmos.

4.2 Axiomatic Systems and Society

  • Benedict de Spinoza (17th-century Dutch philosopher, lens maker) wrote Ethics (1677) in axiomatic form: definitions, axioms and 36 propositions in Part 1, including the necessary existence of God. He wanted to do for philosophy what Euclid did for geometry.
  • The US Declaration of Independence (“We hold these truths to be self-evident…”) works like a set of axioms. Several models of government could fit them.
  • Any society runs on axioms, written down (like the 1987 Philippine Constitution) or understood. The Supreme Court guards the axiom system, the Constitution.

4.3 Abstraction and Symbols

Abstract comes from Latin for “to pull out of” or “separate from.” Abstraction focuses on the essential features of a situation and sets aside the distracting details. It doesn't mean vague.

Example: from arithmetic to algebra

5·3 = 15 = 16 − 1 (4+1)(4−1) = 4² − 1² 6·2 = 12 = 16 − 4 (4+2)(4−2) = 4² − 2² 7·1 = 7 = 16 − 9 (4+3)(4−3) = 4² − 3² ↓ abstract (x + y)(x − y) = x² − y²

High school algebra is “abstract arithmetic” (classical algebra): letters stand for numbers so the general rule shows.

Operations

An operation on a set S assigns to each ordered pair (x, y) of elements of S exactly one element of S, written x * y. Ordered means (x, y) ≠ (y, x) when x ≠ y. Operations can be shown in an operation table.

One structure, many situations
