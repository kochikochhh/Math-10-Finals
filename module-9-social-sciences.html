1. Voting Theory & Social Choice

Social choice theory combines conflicting individual choices into one group choice. The math underneath is order (order theory). Kenneth Arrow laid the foundations in Social Choice and Individual Values.

  • Voting method = the algorithm that consolidates votes into a winner. It doesn't mean voting machines or precincts.
  • Ranked (preferential) voting: voters rank all candidates, with no ties.
  • Preference schedule: a table of how many voters chose each ranking.
  • Transitivity: if X > Y and Y > Z, then X > Z. Removing candidates shouldn't change relative preferences.
  • Majority means at least 50% + 1 of the votes.

Non-transitivity example (Leni's suitors): A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A, so there's no clear choice. With three equal voter groups ranking ABC, BCA and CAB, two-thirds prefer A to B and B to C, yet two-thirds prefer C to A. (The module text says “A to C” here, which is a typo; count the columns to check.)

The four voting methods

MethodHow it works
Plurality (pataasan)Most 1st-place votes wins.
Borda countWith N candidates: 1st = N points, 2nd = N−1, …, last = 1. Highest total wins. (Jean-Charles de Borda)
Pairwise comparisonsEvery head-to-head match-up earns the winner 1 point. Most points wins.
Plurality with eliminationDrop the candidate with the fewest 1st-place votes; repeat until someone has a majority.

1.3 Fairness Criteria

CriterionMeaning
CondorcetA candidate who beats every other candidate head-to-head should win. (Marquis de Condorcet)
MajorityA candidate with a majority (50% + 1) of 1st-place votes should win.
MonotonicityIf X wins and a re-vote only changes in X's favor, X should still win.
Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA)If X wins and losing candidates are removed and votes recounted, X should still win.
UnanimityIf everyone prefers one option to another, so should society.
Non-dictatorshipThe result shouldn't just follow one person's preferences.

Arrow's Impossibility Theorem (1952): no ranked voting system with three or more candidates can always satisfy monotonicity, IIA, unanimity and non-dictatorship together. There is no ideal voting method. Arrow won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1972.

Approval voting is an alternative: voters approve as many candidates as they like, with no ranking. Supporters say it's simple, flexible, elects strong candidates, isn't affected by the number of candidates, reduces negative campaigning and doesn't greatly hurt minority candidates. It suits smaller groups.

2. Weighted Voting & Power

In a weighted voting system, voters don't have equal votes. Examples: shareholders (one vote per share), bloc voting in legislatures, committees where the chair breaks ties.

Notation: [q : w₁, w₂, …, wN], where q is the quota (the minimum votes to pass a motion) and the weights are listed largest first. A valid quota satisfies ½(total) ≤ q ≤ total. (Strictly, it must be more than half, or both sides could win.)

TermMeaningExample
DictatorWeight ≥ quota; can pass anything alone.P1 in [6: 7, 3, 1, 1]
DummyNever critical; has no power.P4 in [51: 26, 26, 26, 22]
Veto powerNot a dictator, but every winning coalition needs them.P1 in [8: 7, 3, 1, 1]
CoalitionA group voting together. Its weight is the sum of its votes. N players give 2N − 1 coalitions.The grand coalition has everyone.
Critical playerLeaving turns a winning coalition into a losing one.In {P1, P3} of [51: 48, 47, 5], both are critical.
More votes doesn't always mean more power. In [51: 48, 47, 5], all three players have equal power (1/3 each), since any two of them win.

Banzhaf power index (John Banzhaf, 1965)

  1. List all coalitions (2N − 1).
  2. Keep the winning ones.
  3. Mark the critical players in each.
  4. CP = number of times player P is critical.
  5. T = total critical count over all players.
Banzhaf index of P = CP / T

Example: [4: 3, 2, 1]. Winning coalitions: {A,B} (A and B critical), {A,C} (A and C critical), {A,B,C} (only A critical). A = 3, B = 1, C = 1, T = 5, so the distribution is (3/5, 1/5, 1/5).

Results to remember

  • If q = the total (a unanimous vote is needed), every player has power 1/N.
  • Proportional systems like [9: 4,4,4,4] and [27: 12,12,12,12] have the same distribution.
  • Any 3-player system with no veto power gives (1/3, 1/3, 1/3).
  • Tolle (2003): a 4-player system with no veto power has only five possible distributions: (1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4), (5/12,1/4,1/4,1/12), (1/2,1/6,1/6,1/6), (1/3,1/3,1/6,1/6), (1/3,1/3,1/3,0). None is strictly decreasing.
  • Chair breaks 2–2 ties in a 4-member committee: (1/2, 1/6, 1/6, 1/6). In a 5-member committee where the chair only votes on ties: everyone gets 1/5.
  • UN Security Council [39: 7,7,7,7,7, 1×10]: each permanent member has 16.7%, each non-permanent member 1.65%.

US Electoral College

Each state's weight = 2 senators + its representatives. DC counts as a state with 3 votes. Total 538; quota 270. California has the most (55 votes, 11.36% power). In 2000, Bush won 271–266 in electoral votes while Gore won the popular vote. In 2016, Trump won the Electoral College while Clinton won the popular vote by about 2.86 million.

3. Fair Division & Apportionment

Fair division: split a set of goods among n players so each thinks their share is worth at least 1/n of the total, by their own values. A fair division scheme is decisive, internal (no outside judge), assumes players don't know each other's values, and assumes players are rational.

  • Continuous goods: land, cake. Discrete goods: cars, houses, jewelry. “I cut, you choose” is a continuous scheme.
  • Story: 17 camels split 1/2, 1/3, 1/9. The wise man adds his own camel to make 18, gives 9, 6 and 2 (= 17), then takes his camel back.
  • Apportionment: dividing identical, indivisible things (like congressional seats) among parties with unequal shares.
TermFormula / meaning
Standard divisor (SD)Total population ÷ number of seats
Standard quota (SQ)State population ÷ SD
Lower / upper quotaSQ rounded down / up
Quota ruleEach state should get its lower or upper quota.
Modified divisor (MD)Found by trial and error so the rounded quotas add up to the seat total exactly.

Quota methods

  • Hamilton (1791): give lower quotas, then hand out the leftover seats by largest fractional part.
  • Lowndes (1822): same, but rank by relative fractional part = fractional part ÷ integer part. Example: 41.82 → 0.82/41 = 0.02.

Example (populations 657k, 237k, 106k; 100 seats): Hamilton gives 66, 24, 10. Lowndes gives 65, 24, 11.

Paradoxes (they affect the quota methods)

Alabama paradoxAdding seats makes a state lose a seat.
Population paradoxA state's population grows, yet it loses a seat.
New-state paradoxAdding a new state with its fair share changes other states' seats.

Balinski–Young Impossibility Theorem (1980): a method that never violates the quota rule must produce paradoxes, and a method with no paradoxes must violate the quota rule. No apportionment method is perfect.

Fun fact: the first US presidential veto was over math. In 1792, George Washington vetoed Hamilton's apportionment bill, and Jefferson's method was used instead. (The module says 1791; the veto itself was in April 1792.)
