Introduction

Plato: “Let no one enter, who is ignorant of Geometry.” The word geometry comes from two Greek words: geo (earth) and metron (measurement). Early civilizations developed it on their own as a practical way of dealing with areas, lengths and volumes.

1. Before Euclid

BabyloniansClay tablets400 tablets in cuneiform script, unearthed in the 1850s. Gave formulas for areas and volumes (circles, cylinders). Circumference ≈ 3 × diameter, so they approximated π ≈ 3. They had Pythagorean triples, but no source shows they knew the theorem itself.
EgyptiansAhmes / Rhind PapyrusArea and volume formulas used to build the pyramids and work out food supply.
GreekEratosthenesFirst to calculate the circumference of the earth, very close to the modern value.
GreekPythagorasRight triangle: a² + b² = c², where c is opposite the right angle.
GreekArchimedes of SyracuseVolumes of irregular shapes; accurate approximation of π using the method of exhaustion (developed by Eudoxus of Cnidus).
GreekIrrational numbersThe Greeks discovered √2, π and the golden ratio (divine proportion), φ.

The golden ratio

Divide a segment of length 1 into a longer part x and a shorter part 1 − x so that whole : longer = longer : shorter:

1/x = x/(1 − x) → x² + x − 1 = 0 → x = (−1 + √5)/2 ≈ 0.618…

The value is non-repeating and non-terminating. Its reciprocal is 1.618…. Rectangles whose length-to-width ratio is the golden ratio are golden rectangles, often called the most pleasing proportions.

Heads-up: the module calls 0.618 “φ” and 1.618 its reciprocal. Most textbooks do it the other way around: φ = 1.618… and 1/φ = 0.618…. For the exam, follow your instructor's convention. Either way, the two numbers differ by exactly 1.

2. Euclidean Geometry

Euclid of Alexandria (the “father of geometry”) described his system in the textbook The Elements. It is the oldest geometry, so for more than a thousand years nobody needed to call it “Euclidean.” Many results came from earlier mathematicians. Euclid was the first to fit them into one deductive, logical system.

Undefined terms: point, line, plane.

2.1 Axioms (common notions)

  • Things equal to the same thing are equal to each other.
  • If equals are added to equals, the wholes are equal.
  • If equals are subtracted from equals, the remainders are equal.
  • Things that coincide with one another are equal to one another.
  • The whole is greater than the part.

2.2 The five postulates

#Postulate
1A straight line can be drawn from any point to any point.
2A finite straight line can be extended continuously in a straight line.
3A circle can be drawn with any center and any radius.
4All right angles are equal.
5If a transversal makes interior angles on one side summing to less than two right angles (< 180°), the two lines meet on that side. This is the Parallel Postulate.

Postulates 1 to 4 seemed obvious. The 5th was long and complicated, and many thought it could be proven from the others, but no one could. They only produced equivalent statements:

Posidonius100 BC“Two lines are equidistant.”

Playfair19th centuryPlayfair's Axiom (most popular): through a point P not on line l, there is exactly one line through P parallel to l.

A plane models Euclidean geometry.

Triangles in Euclidean geometry

  • Interior angles sum to exactly 180°.
  • Congruent = same size and shape. Criteria: SSS (three sides), SAS (two sides and the included angle), ASA (two angles and the included side).
  • Similar = same shape, possibly different size; corresponding angles are equal.
  • Congruent triangles are always similar, but similar triangles need not be congruent.

3. Non-Euclidean Geometries

Activity 2 (tearing off a triangle's three angles to form a straight line) shows the Euclidean 180°. Mathematicians then asked two “what if” questions about Playfair's Axiom. Trying to prove the 5th postulate gave rise to new, equally consistent geometries.

3.1 Hyperbolic geometry: “what if there is more than one parallel?”

  • Studied in the early 1800s by Nikolai Lobachevsky (Russian), János Bolyai (Hungarian) and Carl Friedrich Gauss (German). Gauss was first but never published, so Lobachevsky and Bolyai got the credit.
  • Lobachevsky published first (1829, Geometrical Researches on the Theory of Parallels); Bolyai three years later (1832).
  • Model: the Poincaré disk (Henri Poincaré). Points are inside the disk, not on the boundary. Lines are diameters or arcs that meet the boundary at right angles.
  • Postulate: through P not on l, there are at least two lines parallel to l.
  • Triangle angle sum is less than 180°. Similar triangles are congruent.

3.2 Elliptic geometry: “what if there is no parallel?”

  • Studied deeply by Bernhard Riemann (1826–1866).
  • Model: a sphere (globe). Lines are great circles, the circles of maximum diameter, such as the equator or any longitude line. Every great circle through the North Pole hits the equator.
  • Postulate: through P not on l, there is no line parallel to l. All lines intersect.
  • Triangle angle sum is more than 180°. Similar triangles are congruent.
  • Used in sea and air navigation for distances and flight paths.

4. Projective Geometry

  • Gérard Desargues, French mathematician and engineer, is the father of projective geometry. He treated parallel lines as meeting at an ideal point, so any two lines in a plane intersect.
  • Triangles ABC and abc are perspective from a point P if lines Aa, Bb, Cc meet at P, the center of perspectivity.
  • They are perspective from a line l if corresponding sides meet at points on l, the axis of perspectivity.
Desargues' Theorem: if two triangles are perspective from a point, then they are perspective from a line.

Projective geometry studies vanishing points and perspective. Renaissance artists used it to paint 3D-looking scenes. Today it helps create 3D images in games like Pac-Man and Super Mario Bros., and appears in machine vision (Mundy & Zisserman).

5. Topology

Space came to be seen as a set of points plus abstract relations among them. Henri Poincaré published a systematic treatment in 1895 (the module cites Vorstudien zur Topologie). Topology was the work of many: Cantor, Euler, Fréchet, Hausdorff, Möbius and Riemann.

  • Topological transformations are allowed: stretching, shrinking, twisting and bending, as long as points stay distinct.
  • Not allowed: cutting, tearing, puncturing or merging points.
  • Two figures are topologically equivalent if one can be deformed into the other this way. A circle, a square and a triangle are equivalent, like a square rubber band stretched into a triangle.
  • A coffee cup and a doughnut are equivalent (each has one hole). A coffee cup and a muffin are not.

Activity 4: the Möbius strip

Give a paper strip a half twist and tape the ends (A to D, B to C). A line drawn down the middle covers “both sides” without lifting the pen, because the strip has only one side. Cutting along that center line gives one larger loop, not two.

Big ideas

  • Euclidean geometry was in use (Egypt, Babylon) long before Euclid formalized it.
  • We can build many consistent mathematical systems from different assumptions.
  • Riemannian geometry (non-Euclidean) gave the framework for Einstein's general relativity about forty years later.
  • Bolyai: “Out of nothing, I have created a strange new universe.”
