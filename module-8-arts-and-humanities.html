Introduction

At the core of both math and art lies the concept of patterns. Art uses symmetries and transformations that move an object along a line or in the plane without changing its size or shape.

Math shows up in art in two ways: (1) its concepts are used as artistic tools, and (2) math becomes the major subject of the artwork.

1. Mathematical Music

Beethoven, despite hearing impairment, relied on mathematical patterns to compose. The TED-Ed lesson on the Moonlight Sonata analyzes consonance and dissonance using sine waves (the sine function), comparing the frequency ratios of notes: simple ratios sound consonant, complex ones dissonant.

Rhythm is a combination of sounds and spaces. (Drummer Ndugu Chancler, “The Rhythm Track”)

Dr. James Stewart (author of the best-selling Calculus books, also a violinist) said music relates to math in four aspects: rhythm, harmony, melody and form. He spoke at the MAA Distinguished Lecture Series in 2010, using Beethoven's Fifth Symphony to show these counterparts:

Memory tip: horizontal axis flips pitch up/down (inversion); vertical axis flips time left/right (retrograde). Doing both = 180° rotation.

Composers use these structure-preserving transformations to balance unity and variation, as in J.S. Bach's fugues and canons. Douglas R. Hofstadter (Gödel, Escher, Bach) drew inspiration from Bach's canon of two complementary, reversed lines.

Crab canon

A canon where the theme is played backwards in time, named after the backward walk of crabs. Bach's first canon from The Musical Offering (1747) plays the melody forward and its retrograde (reflection about a vertical axis) at the same time. Written out, the score joins end to start, which is why it is often shown on a Möbius strip.

The Möbius strip is a surface with only one side and one edge. The recycling symbol is a variant. Escher's Moebius Strip II (1963) is a woodcut in red, black and grey-green, printed from 3 blocks.

2. The Math of Dance

Dance can be described as geometry in motion.

Math Dance pioneers: Dr. Karl Schaffer (dancer, choreographer, mathematician), Erik Stern (choreographer, dancer, musician, educator) and Scott Kim (mathematician, graphic artist, puzzle master). They note that math and dance share symmetry, spatial awareness, counting problems and patterns, and that both creatively explore patterns in space and time.

Precision in dance: critic Louis Horst insisted a quarter of an inch makes a difference. Carole de la Paz-Zialcita recalls how National Artist for Dance Leonor Orosa Goquingco drilled dancers so every arm was at the same angle; precision was kept by muscle memory (first tour abroad: 1961).

3. Weaving Culture

Philippine weaving (textiles, fabric, mats, baskets, ornaments, wall decorations) is full of symmetry and repeated motifs. The module asks: what mathematical concepts are involved in weaving?

What the module says

  • Dr. Ma. Louise Antonette N. De Las Peñas spoke on mathematical symmetries of indigenous textiles in the Floy Quintos collection.
  • Exhibit: Woven Universes: Math, Method, Meaning, and Magic in Philippine Indigenous Textiles, Yuchengco Museum, February 21, 2015.
  • The UP Sablay is woven by weavers of Arevalo, Iloilo using the sinuksok method. Activity 9: spot its symmetries, then design your own symmetric motif.
  • The references also cite studies of binakol patterns, inabel textiles, and the “algebra of weaving patterns” of the Kankana-ey of Mountain Province.

The math inside a weave

  • A weave is a grid of 0s and 1s. In each cell, either the warp is on top (1) or the weft is (0). Plain weave is a checkerboard, and twill shifts the pattern one step each row, which makes diagonals.
  • Repeat unit: the smallest block that, copied by translation, makes the whole cloth.
  • Strip borders on sashes and hems are frieze patterns, which repeat in one direction (Module 5 has the seven types).
  • All-over patterns are wallpaper patterns, which repeat in two directions. UP's weaving-mathematics project classifies Ilocano textiles by symmetry group. The sinan-paddak ti pusa (“cat's paw”) binakol design is pmm: mirror lines both ways, meeting at 180° rotation centers. A Vigan binakol variant is p1, which has translations only.
  • A single Paoay flower motif has four symmetries: do nothing, a 180° turn, a horizontal flip and a vertical flip. Together these form a group called the Klein four-group.

4. Architecture

  • San Miguel Corporation head office: designed by Francisco Mañosa with brothers Jose and Manuel; landscaping by Ildefonso Santos (2006 National Artist for Architecture). Inspired by the Banaue Rice Terraces.
  • Robin Morgan noted the terraces' planting patterns: whirls, nested triangles, concentric circles, Greek key motifs, the same patterns on Ifugao hems, sleeves and tattoos.
  • Dr. James Stewart: calculus is the mathematics of curves.
  • Integral House (Toronto, Canada): a four-story, curve-dominated house built for Stewart, designed by Shim-Sutcliffe; named after the calculus integral.
  • Lurie Garden at Millennium Park, Chicago: landscape architects used geometry to win the design competition.

5. Poems with Patterns

William James: the ideal is the union of the mathematician with the poet, “fervor with measure, passion with correctness.”

Filipino poetic forms: Balagtasan (Tagalog), dallot (Ilocano), ambahan (Mindoro), and spoken word.

Haiku

Japanese, not primarily rhyming. 3 lines with 5-7-5 syllables.

5 I don't understand! 7 No matter how near I am, 5 I can't approach you. — “Asymptotes”

“Thirteen Haikus” by UP Los Baños students (Leslie Anne Garcia, Anna Jane DeLima, Julie Ann Medrano): the first letters of the 13 titles spell COMBINATORICS.

Fibonacci poem

Lines follow the Fibonacci sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13…, counting either words or syllables.

11235813853211
  • Andrew Jackson counted words (1, 1, 2, 3): “One / person / with courage / makes a majority.”
  • Oliver Wendell Holmes, Jr. counted syllables (1, 1, 2, 3, 5): “Life / is / painting / a picture / not doing a sum.”
  • Longer poems may go up then back down (1, 1, 2, 3, 5 … 3, 2, 1, 1), like a reflection, as in Dr. Nat Friedman's “Stacking” and his poem for thesis adviser Rafael Chacon (Brown University). Or repeat: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8.
